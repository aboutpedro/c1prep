Present tenses

Each set has 10 sentences. Follow the instructions above the sentences, then press Check answers.

At B2 you already know the present tenses. What the exam checks is whether you choose the right one: the simple or the continuous, and the present perfect or the past simple. Here are the differences that matter most.

Present simple or present continuous?

present simplepresent continuous
facts and habits: Water boils at 100°C. I cycle to work.now or around now: I’m trying to concentrate.
permanent situations: She lives in Lisbon.temporary situations: She’s staying with us this week.
timetables: The train leaves at 7.15.changing situations: Prices are rising.

You might also see

  • He’s always borrowing my charger without asking!always + continuous = a habit that annoys you
  • I usually work from home, but this week I’m working in the office.a normal habit and a temporary change

State verbs

Verbs for thoughts, feelings, senses and possession are normally used in the simple, even when we mean “now”: know, believe, understand, want, need, prefer, like, love, hate, belong, own, seem, mean, consist.

  • I don’t understand this question.not I’m not understanding
  • This bag belongs to Laura.

Some verbs have two meanings: a state (simple) and an action (continuous).

verbstateaction
thinkI think you’re right. (opinion)I’m thinking about it. (considering)
haveThey have two cats. (own)We’re having lunch. (eating)
seeI see what you mean. (understand)I’m seeing Ana tonight. (meeting)
tasteIt tastes salty. (has a flavour)She’s tasting the sauce. (trying)
beHe is rude. (always)He’s being rude. (today, behaving)

Present perfect simple or continuous?

Both connect the past to now. The simple focuses on the result or on how many; the continuous focuses on the activity and how long.

The basics

  • I’ve written three emails this morning.result: how many
  • I’ve been writing emails all morning.activity: how long
  • You look tired. Have you been working late?an activity that explains what we see now

You might also see

  • I’ve known her since school.State verbs stay in the simple, even with for or since.
  • How long have you been learning Japanese? / How long have you had this car?

Present perfect or past simple?

Use the past simple when the time is finished (yesterday, in 2019, ago, when I was a child). Use the present perfect when the time reaches up to now or isn’t said.

  • I’ve lost my phone. / I lost my phone at a concert last year.
  • She’s written two novels. / Shakespeare wrote 37 plays.She is still writing; Shakespeare’s life is over.
  • This is the first time I’ve driven on the left.It’s / This is the first time + present perfect
fora length of time: for two years, for ages
sincea starting point: since 2019, since I was ten
already / justbefore the main verb: I’ve already eaten. She’s just left.
yetquestions and negatives, at the end: Have you finished yet?
stillbefore a negative auxiliary: He still hasn’t replied.

Careful: no present perfect with a finished time: I saw him yesterday, not I have seen him yesterday.
