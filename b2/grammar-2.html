Past tenses, used to and would

Each set has 10 sentences. Follow the instructions above the sentences, then press Check answers.

When you tell a story or describe the past, the tense shows the order of events and what was background. You also have three ways to talk about past habits: the past simple, used to and would.

Narrative tenses

past simplethe main events, in order: She opened the door, looked around and went in.
past continuousbackground, or an action in progress: The sun was shining. I was walking home when…
past perfectsomething that happened before another past event: The film had already started.
past perfect continuousan activity that went on up to a past moment: Her eyes were red. She had been crying.

You might also see

  • By the time we arrived, everyone had left.
  • It was the first time I’d ever seen snow.It was the first time + past perfect
  • We’d been walking for hours when we found a café.how long, up to that moment

Careful: you don’t need the past perfect when the order is already clear: As soon as she heard the news, she called her parents.

Past perfect simple or continuous?

The same difference as in the present perfect: the simple for a result or a number, the continuous for an activity and how long it lasted.

  • By midnight I’d written four pages.result
  • He was out of breath because he’d been running.activity with a visible effect
  • I’d known Sam for years before I met his brother.state verb: simple

used to and would

Both describe past habits that are no longer true. Only used to works for past states.

actionsstates (be, have, live, like…)
used to✓ We used to go fishing.✓ I used to have long hair.
would✓ Every summer we’d go fishing.✗ I would have long hair.
past simple✓ We went fishing every summer.✓ I had long hair.

Use the past simple, not used to, for a single event or a number of times: I went to the cinema three times last week.

Careful: in questions and negatives there’s no -d: Did you use to…? I didn’t use to…

be used to and get used to

be used to = something is normal for you. get used to = it is becoming normal. Both are followed by a noun or an -ing form, because to here is a preposition.

  • I’m used to working at night.
  • It took me months to get used to wearing glasses.
  • You’ll soon get used to the weather.

Careful: I used to drive (a past habit) is very different from I’m used to driving (it’s normal for me).
