Relative clauses

Each set has 10 sentences. Follow the instructions above the sentences, then press Check answers.

A relative clause gives more information about a noun. It starts with a word like who, which, that, whose, where or when, and lets you join two short sentences into one.

Joining two sentences

wordused forexample
whopeoplethe woman who lives next door
whichthings and animalsthe car which I bought
thatpeople or things (not after a comma)the film that won the prize
whosepossession: his, her, theirthe boy whose bike was stolen
whereplacesthe town where I grew up
whentimesthe day when we met

Careful: the relative word replaces he, she, it or them, so don’t repeat them: the film that I saw, not the film that I saw it.

Defining clauses: which one?

These clauses tell us exactly which person or thing we mean. Without them, the sentence isn’t clear. There are no commas, and you can use that instead of who or which.

The basics

  • The man who lives next door is a pilot.
  • Where are the keys that were on the table?
  • That’s the woman whose son won the race.

Leaving out the relative word

When who, which or that is the object of the clause (there’s another subject after it), you can leave it out. This is very common in speech.

  • The book (that) you lent me is great.You is the subject, so that can go.
  • The man who called you is here.Who is the subject, so it must stay.

You might also see

  • Is this the person you were talking about?The preposition often goes at the end.
  • That’s the hotel where we stayed last year.

Non-defining clauses: extra information

These clauses add extra information. We already know who or what we mean, so the sentence still makes sense without them. They go between commas, and you can’t use that or leave out the relative word.

The basics

  • My brother, who lives in Berlin, is a chef.
  • The Eiffel Tower, which was built in 1889, is 330 metres tall.

You might also see

  • He didn’t call me back, which was a bit rude.Which can refer to the whole idea before it.
  • We visited Porto, where my grandparents were born.

Careful: the commas change the meaning. My sister who lives in London is a nurse suggests I have more than one sister. My sister, who lives in London, is a nurse suggests I have only one.
