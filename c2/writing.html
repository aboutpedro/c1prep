Text 1: The price of popularity

Tourism has brought undeniable benefits to many historic cities, funding the restoration of buildings and providing employment for thousands. Yet in the most popular destinations, these gains have come at a heavy cost. As apartments are turned into short-term holiday lets, rents rise and long-standing residents are forced out, until the old centre becomes a stage set, crowded by day and deserted by night. A city that exists only for visitors is no longer a city at all. Unless numbers are actively limited, the very qualities that attract tourists will be destroyed by them.

Text 2: A door that should stay open

Calls to restrict tourism are understandable, but they deserve more scrutiny than they usually receive. Caps on visitor numbers and steep entry fees tend to favour the wealthy, turning travel back into the privilege it was a century ago. Travel, moreover, does more than generate income: at its best, it breaks down prejudice and allows people to see their own lives from a new perspective. The problems of overcrowding are real, but they are problems of management, not of tourism itself. Better planning, not closed doors, is the answer.

Text 1: Everyone an artist

For most of history, creative work has been the preserve of those with the time and money to master difficult techniques. Artificial intelligence is changing that. Someone with no training can now produce an illustration, compose a piece of music or draft a story in minutes, and many find the experience liberating. Critics complain that this cheapens creativity, but similar fears greeted photography and the home computer. Tools do not replace imagination; they give it new ways of finding expression. The real question is not whether people should use them, but what they will choose to make.

Text 2: The value of struggle

What is lost when a machine does the hard part? Anyone who has learned to draw or play an instrument knows that the difficulty is not an obstacle to creativity but its source: it is in wrestling with the material that we discover what we actually want to say. A tool that produces a polished result instantly may be useful, but it removes the very process through which skills, and ideas, develop. There is a risk that a generation will grow up able to generate endless content but unable to create anything genuinely its own.

Text 1: A preparation for life

It has become fashionable to regard competition in schools as harmful, but this view does students few favours. Rankings, prizes and examinations give young people clear goals and the satisfaction of seeing their efforts rewarded. More importantly, they reflect the world that awaits them, in which jobs, university places and promotions are allocated competitively. Sheltering students from this reality does not make it disappear; it simply leaves them unprepared. Learning to win graciously, and to lose without giving up, is among the most valuable lessons a school can teach.

Text 2: Learning, not winning

When school becomes a competition, the goal quietly shifts from learning to winning. Students learn to avoid difficult subjects in which they might perform badly, to see classmates as rivals rather than collaborators, and to measure their own worth by their position in a list. For the few at the top, this may be motivating; for the majority, it is discouraging, and for some, a source of real anxiety. Research suggests that students who work together learn more, not less. Schools should be preparing young people to cooperate, which most workplaces now demand.

Text 1: Room to live

Our cities face a severe housing shortage, and the solution is obvious, if unpopular: we must build more homes, and build them more densely. Every hectare reserved for lawns and ornamental parks is a hectare on which families could be living. Low-density cities also force people into long car journeys, with all the pollution that brings. A compact city, in which homes, shops and workplaces are close together, is better for the environment and fairer to those who cannot afford spacious suburbs. Green space is pleasant, but it is a luxury we can no longer afford.

Text 2: The city's lungs

Parks are often treated as a decorative extra, the first thing to be sacrificed when land is needed for something more 'useful'. This is a profound mistake. Green spaces cool overheated streets, absorb rainwater that would otherwise flood them, and provide a habitat for wildlife. Above all, a growing body of evidence links access to nature with lower rates of stress and depression. In crowded districts, where many people have no garden of their own, a local park may be the only green space residents ever see. To build over it is to make city life poorer for everyone.

Text 1: With fame comes responsibility

Celebrities may not have asked to be role models, but they are role models all the same. Millions of young people follow their every move online, copying their clothes, their language and, inevitably, their attitudes. Someone with that kind of influence has an obligation to use it wisely: to avoid glamorising dangerous behaviour, to treat others with respect and to think before they speak. Fame brings enormous rewards, and it is not unreasonable to expect something in return. Those who find the responsibility too heavy are free to step out of the spotlight.

Text 2: Not their job

Every few weeks, a famous footballer or singer is criticised for failing to set a good example, as though being talented at one thing made a person responsible for the moral education of other people's children. This expectation is both unfair and unrealistic. Celebrities are ordinary human beings, with ordinary flaws, whose private lives are exposed to a scrutiny that few of us could withstand. The people who genuinely shape young people's values are parents, teachers and friends. Placing that burden on strangers lets those closer to home off far too lightly.

Part 2 · Choose one task

Article. You see this announcement in an international magazine:

The skill nobody taught me

We are publishing a series of articles about skills that people have learned outside school or university. Tell us about a skill you acquired in this way. How did you learn it, and why has it proved more valuable to you than much of what you were formally taught?

Write your article.

Article. An international website is running a series called 'Cities in transition'. Write an article for the series about a town or city you know well. You should describe the most significant ways in which it has changed over the past twenty years, evaluate whether these changes have improved the lives of the people who live there, and suggest what the place may be like in twenty years' time.

Write your article.

Letter. You have read an article in a national newspaper which claimed that young people today read less, know less about current affairs and are less able to concentrate than previous generations, mainly because of social media. Write a letter to the editor responding to the article. You should explain to what extent you agree with the writer's claims and suggest what, if anything, should be done.

Write your letter.

Letter. A charitable foundation is offering grants to individuals who want to set up projects that bring different generations together in their local community. Write a letter to the foundation applying for a grant. You should describe the project you have in mind, explain why it is needed in your area, and convince the foundation that it would be a worthwhile use of its money.

Write your letter.

Report. You work for an international company that introduced a training programme for new staff a year ago. Your manager has asked you to write a report on the programme. Your report should assess how effective the programme has been, identify any problems that new employees have experienced, and recommend changes that would improve it.

Write your report.

Report. Your local council is reviewing how it supports cultural life in the area and has asked residents to submit reports. Write a report in which you evaluate the cultural facilities currently available (such as theatres, cinemas, museums and libraries), explain why some groups of people make little use of them, and recommend how the council should invest in the future.

Write your report.

Review. A literary magazine has invited readers to review a book, either fiction or non-fiction, that deals with an important social issue. In your review, you should briefly outline the book and the issue it addresses, evaluate how effectively it deals with that issue, and say whether you think it could change readers' views.

Write your review.

Review. An arts and culture website is publishing reviews of museums, galleries and historic sites. Write a review of one you have visited recently. You should describe how the collection or site is presented, compare the experience with what you had expected, and assess which kinds of visitor would get the most out of a visit.

Write your review.
