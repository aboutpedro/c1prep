Writing · Parts 1 and 2

Writing tasks

In the exam you have 1 hour 30 minutes for both parts: one essay (Part 1) and one task you choose (Part 2). Write 240–280 words for the essay and 280–320 words for Part 2. Your text stays in this browser as you type, so you can come back to it later.

How your writing is marked, and how to check it yourself

In C2 Proficiency, your writing is marked on four things. Read your text again and ask yourself:

Content
Did I deal with everything the task asks, with ideas that are fully developed and relevant? In Part 1, did I summarise and evaluate the key points of both texts?
Communicative achievement
Is the register and tone exactly right for the reader, used with confidence and flexibility? Would the text have the effect I want?
Organisation
Is it a coherent whole, with a wide range of cohesive devices used naturally and without fuss?
Language
Did I use a wide range of sophisticated vocabulary and grammar with precision and style? Are mistakes rare and hard to notice?

Then compare your text with the model answer under each task. A different answer can be just as good: what matters is that it does the job well.

Write an essay summarising and evaluating the key points from both texts. Use your own words throughout as far as possible, and include your own ideas in your answer.

Text 1: The price of popularity

Tourism has brought undeniable benefits to many historic cities, funding the restoration of buildings and providing employment for thousands. Yet in the most popular destinations, these gains have come at a heavy cost. As apartments are turned into short-term holiday lets, rents rise and long-standing residents are forced out, until the old centre becomes a stage set, crowded by day and deserted by night. A city that exists only for visitors is no longer a city at all. Unless numbers are actively limited, the very qualities that attract tourists will be destroyed by them.

Text 2: A door that should stay open

Calls to restrict tourism are understandable, but they deserve more scrutiny than they usually receive. Caps on visitor numbers and steep entry fees tend to favour the wealthy, turning travel back into the privilege it was a century ago. Travel, moreover, does more than generate income: at its best, it breaks down prejudice and allows people to see their own lives from a new perspective. The problems of overcrowding are real, but they are problems of management, not of tourism itself. Better planning, not closed doors, is the answer.

Show a model answer

One good way to answer this task (257 words). Write your own answer first, then compare.

Both texts address the growing tension between the economic benefits of tourism and the damage it can inflict on the places people most want to visit, yet they reach strikingly different conclusions about what should be done.

The first writer acknowledges that tourism has paid for restoration and created jobs, but argues that in the most visited cities these gains are outweighed by the displacement of residents. As homes become holiday rentals, local people are priced out and historic centres lose their everyday life, becoming little more than attractive backdrops. For this writer, only firm limits on visitor numbers can prevent tourism from destroying its own appeal.

The second text, by contrast, warns that such limits would penalise ordinary travellers, restoring an era when only the affluent could see the world. It also stresses that travel has value beyond economics, broadening minds and challenging prejudice. Overcrowding, it contends, stems from poor management rather than from tourism as such.

To my mind, each writer captures part of the truth. The first is right that the housing crisis in popular cities is real and cannot be wished away, while the second rightly points out that blunt caps and high fees are socially regressive. The most convincing solution, I would argue, lies between the two: regulating short-term rentals to protect housing, and spreading visitors more evenly across seasons and destinations, would address the root causes without closing the door on anyone.

Ultimately, tourism need not be curtailed, but it must be governed far more intelligently than it is at present.

Why it works

  • The introduction frames the debate in one sentence, without repeating either text.
  • Each summary paraphrases the key points closely but in new words (priced out, little more than attractive backdrops, socially regressive).
  • The evaluation weighs both sides and arrives at a clear, reasoned position of the writer’s own.
  • C2 control of register and cohesion: by contrast, as such, To my mind, Ultimately.

Text 1: Everyone an artist

For most of history, creative work has been the preserve of those with the time and money to master difficult techniques. Artificial intelligence is changing that. Someone with no training can now produce an illustration, compose a piece of music or draft a story in minutes, and many find the experience liberating. Critics complain that this cheapens creativity, but similar fears greeted photography and the home computer. Tools do not replace imagination; they give it new ways of finding expression. The real question is not whether people should use them, but what they will choose to make.

Text 2: The value of struggle

What is lost when a machine does the hard part? Anyone who has learned to draw or play an instrument knows that the difficulty is not an obstacle to creativity but its source: it is in wrestling with the material that we discover what we actually want to say. A tool that produces a polished result instantly may be useful, but it removes the very process through which skills, and ideas, develop. There is a risk that a generation will grow up able to generate endless content but unable to create anything genuinely its own.

Show a model answer

One good way to answer this task (251 words). Write your own answer first, then compare.

The rapid spread of artificial intelligence into creative fields has provoked a debate about what creativity actually is, and the two texts offer sharply contrasting answers.

The first text celebrates AI as a democratising force. Whereas creative expression once required years of training and the leisure to pursue it, anyone can now produce music, images or stories almost instantly. The writer dismisses claims that this devalues art, comparing them to the anxieties once provoked by photography, and insists that tools merely open new channels for imagination; what matters is how people use them.

The second writer is far more sceptical, arguing that the struggle involved in mastering a craft is not an unfortunate barrier but the very means by which artists discover their ideas. By supplying a finished product effortlessly, AI bypasses this process, and the writer fears a generation capable of producing vast quantities of content yet lacking any authentic creative voice.

While the historical comparison in the first text is persuasive, it overlooks a crucial difference: a camera still requires the photographer to choose, frame and wait, whereas generative tools can make most of those decisions themselves. In this respect, the second writer’s concern seems well founded. That said, the argument risks romanticising difficulty; many people who will never learn to paint may still have something worth expressing.

On balance, I believe AI can enrich creative life, provided it is treated as a starting point for exploration rather than a substitute for the effort through which genuine originality emerges.

Why it works

  • The evaluation goes beyond agreeing or disagreeing: it tests the first text’s analogy and finds a weakness.
  • It also challenges the second text (risks romanticising difficulty), so the final position is genuinely balanced.
  • Sophisticated, precise language: democratising force, bypasses this process, well founded, a substitute for.

Text 1: A preparation for life

It has become fashionable to regard competition in schools as harmful, but this view does students few favours. Rankings, prizes and examinations give young people clear goals and the satisfaction of seeing their efforts rewarded. More importantly, they reflect the world that awaits them, in which jobs, university places and promotions are allocated competitively. Sheltering students from this reality does not make it disappear; it simply leaves them unprepared. Learning to win graciously, and to lose without giving up, is among the most valuable lessons a school can teach.

Text 2: Learning, not winning

When school becomes a competition, the goal quietly shifts from learning to winning. Students learn to avoid difficult subjects in which they might perform badly, to see classmates as rivals rather than collaborators, and to measure their own worth by their position in a list. For the few at the top, this may be motivating; for the majority, it is discouraging, and for some, a source of real anxiety. Research suggests that students who work together learn more, not less. Schools should be preparing young people to cooperate, which most workplaces now demand.

Show a model answer

One good way to answer this task (256 words). Write your own answer first, then compare.

The role of competition in education divides opinion sharply, and the two texts present opposing views on whether it helps or hinders students.

According to the first writer, rankings, prizes and examinations are beneficial because they give students clear targets and a sense of reward for hard work. More fundamentally, the writer claims that adult life is inherently competitive, so protecting young people from competition merely leaves them ill-equipped for the future. Learning to handle both success and failure is presented as one of the most important things school can teach.

The second text argues that competition distorts the purpose of education. When winning becomes the goal, students avoid challenges, regard their peers as opponents and judge themselves by their ranking. While a handful of high achievers may thrive, most are demoralised and some become seriously anxious. Citing research showing that collaboration improves learning, the writer concludes that schools should instead foster the cooperation that modern employers expect.

Both writers make valid points, but I find the second more persuasive. The first text assumes that competition is the only way to prepare students for adult life, yet most workplaces reward teamwork at least as much as individual performance. Moreover, it ignores the cost borne by the majority who never reach the top. Nevertheless, it would be unrealistic to remove competition altogether; students do need to experience setbacks and learn resilience.

In my view, the best approach is to keep competition in its place, in sport or occasional challenges, while making collaboration the foundation of everyday learning.

Why it works

  • The summaries are concise and selective: they keep the argument, not every detail.
  • The evaluation identifies an assumption in the first text, as the checklist suggests.
  • The conclusion is nuanced but decisive: competition has a place, collaboration comes first.

Text 1: Room to live

Our cities face a severe housing shortage, and the solution is obvious, if unpopular: we must build more homes, and build them more densely. Every hectare reserved for lawns and ornamental parks is a hectare on which families could be living. Low-density cities also force people into long car journeys, with all the pollution that brings. A compact city, in which homes, shops and workplaces are close together, is better for the environment and fairer to those who cannot afford spacious suburbs. Green space is pleasant, but it is a luxury we can no longer afford.

Text 2: The city's lungs

Parks are often treated as a decorative extra, the first thing to be sacrificed when land is needed for something more 'useful'. This is a profound mistake. Green spaces cool overheated streets, absorb rainwater that would otherwise flood them, and provide a habitat for wildlife. Above all, a growing body of evidence links access to nature with lower rates of stress and depression. In crowded districts, where many people have no garden of their own, a local park may be the only green space residents ever see. To build over it is to make city life poorer for everyone.

Show a model answer

One good way to answer this task (257 words). Write your own answer first, then compare.

As cities grow, the question of how urban land should be used has become increasingly pressing, and these two texts take opposite sides on whether housing or green space should take priority.

The first writer maintains that the housing shortage can only be solved by building more homes at higher density, even at the expense of parks and gardens. Compact cities, the writer argues, reduce the need for car travel and are more equitable, since sprawling suburbs are affordable only to the better-off. Green space is described as desirable but ultimately a luxury.

The second text rejects this view, arguing that parks are not ornamental but essential infrastructure. They lower temperatures, help prevent flooding and support wildlife, and, crucially, there is mounting evidence that contact with nature benefits mental health. In densely populated areas, where residents often lack private gardens, a park may be their only access to greenery.

Although the first writer is right that low-density development has serious environmental and social costs, the argument rests on a false choice. Density and green space are not mutually exclusive: many of the world’s most liveable cities combine tall, closely packed housing with generous public parks. Indeed, the denser a neighbourhood becomes, the more its residents depend on shared outdoor space, which strengthens the case made in the second text.

I would therefore conclude that cities should build upwards rather than outwards, but protect and even expand their parks, treating green space not as a luxury to be traded away but as part of what makes density tolerable.

Why it works

  • It identifies a false choice in the first argument, a strong evaluative move at C2.
  • The writer’s own idea (the denser the city, the more it needs parks) links the two texts.
  • Varied, precise vocabulary: more equitable, essential infrastructure, mutually exclusive, tolerable.

Text 1: With fame comes responsibility

Celebrities may not have asked to be role models, but they are role models all the same. Millions of young people follow their every move online, copying their clothes, their language and, inevitably, their attitudes. Someone with that kind of influence has an obligation to use it wisely: to avoid glamorising dangerous behaviour, to treat others with respect and to think before they speak. Fame brings enormous rewards, and it is not unreasonable to expect something in return. Those who find the responsibility too heavy are free to step out of the spotlight.

Text 2: Not their job

Every few weeks, a famous footballer or singer is criticised for failing to set a good example, as though being talented at one thing made a person responsible for the moral education of other people's children. This expectation is both unfair and unrealistic. Celebrities are ordinary human beings, with ordinary flaws, whose private lives are exposed to a scrutiny that few of us could withstand. The people who genuinely shape young people's values are parents, teachers and friends. Placing that burden on strangers lets those closer to home off far too lightly.

Show a model answer

One good way to answer this task (261 words). Write your own answer first, then compare.

Whether celebrities should be expected to act as role models is a long-running debate, and the two texts offer sharply opposing perspectives on the question.

The first writer contends that famous people are role models whether they like it or not, since vast numbers of young followers imitate not only their appearance but also their behaviour and values. With such influence, the writer argues, comes a duty to behave responsibly, and given the rewards that fame brings, this is a reasonable price to pay. Anyone unwilling to accept it, the writer suggests, can always withdraw from public life.

The second text dismisses this expectation as both unjust and impractical. Being gifted in sport or music, the writer argues, does not make someone a moral guide, and celebrities are flawed individuals whose lives are subjected to extraordinary scrutiny. It is family, teachers and peers who truly shape young people’s values, and blaming celebrities allows these people to escape responsibility.

The second argument seems to me the stronger, particularly its point that focusing on celebrities can distract from the influence of those closer to home. However, it underestimates the reach of social media, where young people may spend more time with a celebrity’s content than with their own teachers. The first writer’s claim that fame entails obligations is therefore not unreasonable, although the suggestion that unhappy celebrities can simply step away is somewhat glib.

In conclusion, while the primary responsibility for young people’s values must lie with those around them, celebrities who profit from young audiences should accept that their conduct carries consequences.

Why it works

  • Reporting verbs vary naturally: contends, argues, suggests, dismisses.
  • The evaluation is precise, even pointing out a weakness in the tone of an argument (somewhat glib).
  • The final position combines elements of both texts into the writer’s own view.

Article. You see this announcement in an international magazine:

The skill nobody taught me

We are publishing a series of articles about skills that people have learned outside school or university. Tell us about a skill you acquired in this way. How did you learn it, and why has it proved more valuable to you than much of what you were formally taught?

Write your article.

Show a model answer

One good way to answer this task (285 words). Write your own answer first, then compare.

How a broken bicycle taught me to think

I spent fifteen years in classrooms learning to answer questions that other people had already solved. It took a rusty bicycle, abandoned in my grandfather’s garage, to teach me how to solve problems of my own.

I was sixteen, it was the long summer holiday, and I was bored enough to decide to repair it. I had no tools to speak of, no idea what a derailleur was and no one to ask, since my grandfather had long since given up cycling. What I did have was patience, the internet and an alarming willingness to take things apart. For weeks, my evenings were spent covered in grease, watching videos, ordering the wrong parts and, gradually, ordering the right ones.

The day the bike finally worked, I rode it round the block three times, grinning like an idiot, while my grandfather applauded from the gate. But the real reward came later, when I realised what the process had taught me. Mechanics, it turns out, is less about knowing the answers than about knowing how to find them: observing carefully, forming a hypothesis, testing it and accepting, without too much wounded pride, that you were wrong.

School had given me a great deal of knowledge, much of which I still value, but very little practice in not knowing. My education rewarded the correct answer delivered on time; the bicycle rewarded curiosity and persistence. Years later, as a software engineer, I spend most of my days confronting problems that nobody has solved before, and the approach I rely on is the one I learned in that garage.

Formal education taught me what to think. A broken bicycle taught me how.

Why it works

  • The opening contrast (classrooms versus a rusty bicycle) draws the reader in at once.
  • Narrative detail is vivid and humorous (an alarming willingness to take things apart, grinning like an idiot).
  • Reflection and evaluation follow naturally, answering why the skill was more valuable than school.
  • The final two lines echo the opening, giving the article a satisfying shape.

You should describe the most significant ways in which it has changed over the past twenty years, evaluate whether these changes have improved the lives of the people who live there, and suggest what the place may be like in twenty years' time.

Write your article.

Show a model answer

One good way to answer this task (283 words). Write your own answer first, then compare.

Porto: a city that rediscovered itself

Twenty years ago, my home city of Porto was a place most visitors passed through on their way somewhere else. Its riverside was beautiful but crumbling, and many of the grand old buildings in the centre stood empty, their windows broken and their facades stained with decades of neglect.

The transformation since then has been remarkable. Low-cost flights and a wave of international attention turned Porto into one of Europe’s most fashionable destinations almost overnight. Derelict houses have been restored, new restaurants and galleries seem to have opened on every corner, and the metro now connects the centre with the airport and the suburbs, making daily life far easier for commuters.

Whether these changes have improved residents’ lives depends very much on who you ask. Small business owners, hoteliers and young people working in the creative industries have clearly prospered. For many long-standing residents, however, the picture is far less rosy. As apartments in the historic centre have been converted into holiday rentals, rents have soared, and families who lived there for generations have been forced to move to the outskirts. Ironically, the centre is now livelier than ever, yet home to fewer local people.

Predicting the next twenty years is a risky business, but two scenarios seem possible. If the current trend continues unchecked, the old town could become little more than an open-air museum, beautiful and empty. Alternatively, if recent measures to limit holiday lets and build affordable housing prove effective, Porto could become a model of how a city can welcome the world without losing itself.

Which future it chooses will say a great deal about what kind of city it wants to be.

Why it works

  • It covers all three points (change, evaluation, future) in a clear sequence that builds to the conclusion.
  • The evaluation considers different groups of residents rather than giving a single verdict.
  • Stylistic control: far less rosy, Ironically…, without losing itself, and a reflective final line.

Write your letter.

Show a model answer

One good way to answer this task (288 words). Write your own answer first, then compare.

Dear Editor,

I am writing in response to the article in last Sunday’s edition, which claimed that young people today read less, are less informed about current affairs and have shorter attention spans than previous generations, largely as a result of social media.

I would agree that the article raises a legitimate concern. Platforms designed to capture attention for as long as possible inevitably encourage rapid scrolling rather than sustained concentration, and anyone who has watched a teenager switch between apps every few seconds will recognise the pattern the writer describes. As a teacher, I see its effects in my own classroom.

However, I believe the article’s conclusions are overstated. Young people may read fewer printed newspapers, but many follow the news closely through podcasts, newsletters and online video, often from a wider range of sources than their parents ever consulted. Similarly, the claim that they read less ignores the popularity of online fiction communities and the remarkable sales of novels aimed at young adults. What has changed is not so much the amount young people engage with ideas as the forms in which they do so.

Rather than blaming social media alone, we should focus on helping young people use it critically. Schools could make media literacy, including how to evaluate sources and recognise misinformation, a core part of the curriculum, and parents could set an example by managing their own screen time. Technology companies, too, should be required by law to design platforms that do not exploit users’ attention so ruthlessly, particularly when those users are children.

Young people deserve neither blind praise nor blanket condemnation, but an honest conversation about how to make the most of the tools they have inherited.

Yours faithfully,
Ana Ribeiro

Why it works

  • It refers clearly to the article and acknowledges the valid part of its argument before disagreeing.
  • Counter-arguments are supported with specific examples (podcasts, online fiction, young adult novels).
  • Suggestions are realistic and aimed at different groups: schools, parents and technology companies.
  • The tone is measured and persuasive, and the final sentence is memorable.

Dear Sir or Madam,

I am writing to apply for a grant from your foundation to establish Shared Stories, a project that would bring together older residents and teenagers in the Vale district of my town.

The idea is simple. Every week, pairs made up of one older person and one student from the local secondary school would meet at the community library. The students would help their partners with digital skills, such as video-calling relatives or using online services, while the older participants would share memories of the area, which the students would record. Over the course of a year, these recordings would be edited into an online archive and a public exhibition documenting the history of the district in its residents’ own words.

There is a pressing need for such a project in the Vale. Almost a third of its residents are over seventy, and many live alone, often with family far away. At the same time, the area has few facilities for young people, and local schools report that many students feel disconnected from the community they live in. The two generations rarely meet, yet each has exactly what the other lacks.

The grant would cover a part-time coordinator, recording equipment and the costs of the exhibition, a total of £6,000. The library has already offered its rooms free of charge, and the school has agreed to recognise students’ participation as part of their community service programme. I am therefore confident that the project would deliver lasting benefits at a modest cost, and that it could easily be adopted by other neighbourhoods.

Thank you for considering this application. I would be delighted to provide any further information you may require.

Yours faithfully,
Marco Teixeira

Report on the new staff training programme

Introduction
This report evaluates the training programme introduced for new employees a year ago. It assesses the programme’s effectiveness, identifies the problems new staff have encountered and recommends changes. The findings are based on a questionnaire completed by 48 of the 55 employees who joined during the year, together with interviews with their line managers.

Effectiveness of the programme
Overall, the programme has been successful. Over 80% of respondents rated the introductory week as useful or very useful, with the sessions on internal systems and company policies singled out for praise. Managers reported that new recruits now reach full productivity in around two months, compared with roughly three before the programme was introduced. Staff turnover in the first year has also fallen noticeably.

Problems identified
Nevertheless, several shortcomings emerged. The most frequently mentioned was that the content is largely the same for all departments, so technical staff found parts of it irrelevant, while sales staff felt they received too little product knowledge. In addition, colleagues in our overseas offices, who follow the programme online, described the sessions as long and passive, and several said they felt isolated from their peers at headquarters. Finally, once the first week is over, there is no structured follow-up, leaving some employees unsure whom to approach with questions.

Recommendations
In light of these findings, it is recommended that the programme be restructured into a shared core module followed by department-specific training. Online sessions should be shortened and made more interactive, for instance through small-group activities. It would also be advisable to assign each new employee a mentor for their first three months.

Conclusion
These relatively inexpensive changes would build on the programme’s evident success while addressing its main weaknesses.

Why it works

  • The introduction states the aim and the evidence, which gives the findings authority.
  • Effectiveness is assessed with figures and comparisons, not general impressions.
  • Each recommendation clearly answers one of the problems identified.
  • Consistently impersonal register: it is recommended that the programme be restructured, It would also be advisable.

Cultural life in Riverton: current provision and future priorities

Introduction
This report evaluates the cultural facilities currently available in Riverton, considers why certain groups make little use of them and recommends how the council should direct future investment. It draws on a survey of 200 residents conducted through the community newsletter and on conversations with staff at local venues.

Current facilities
Riverton is well served in some respects. The Central Library is modern, busy and highly valued, particularly by families and students, and the Grand Theatre attracts large audiences to its touring productions. However, the town museum has barely changed in twenty years and attracts few repeat visitors, while the closure of the independent cinema last year has left the town with only a multiplex on the ring road.

Groups that make little use of facilities
The survey suggests that two groups are largely absent. Teenagers described the existing venues as aimed at older people and expensive, with theatre tickets often costing more than they can afford. Residents of the outlying estates, meanwhile, cited poor evening bus services as the main obstacle; several said that attending an event in the centre simply was not practical without a car.

Recommendations
The council’s first priority should be access rather than new buildings. Discounted tickets for under-25s and a late bus service on event nights would remove the most frequently mentioned barriers at relatively low cost. Secondly, the museum would benefit from investment in interactive and temporary exhibitions to encourage repeat visits. In the longer term, the council could explore reopening the former cinema as a community-run venue screening films and hosting local performances.

Conclusion
Riverton does not lack cultural assets; the challenge is ensuring that all its residents can and want to use them.

Why it works

  • Facilities are evaluated with both strengths and weaknesses, using specific examples.
  • The reasons why some groups stay away are supported by evidence from the survey.
  • Recommendations are prioritised (first priority, Secondly, In the longer term), as the checklist asks.
  • The conclusion sums up the argument in a single, memorable sentence.

In your review, you should briefly outline the book and the issue it addresses, evaluate how effectively it deals with that issue, and say whether you think it could change readers' views.

Write your review.

Show a model answer

One good way to answer this task (293 words). Write your own answer first, then compare.

The Quiet Room: a novel that refuses easy answers

Few novels manage to tackle loneliness without either wallowing in it or offering a tidy cure. Clara Mendes’s The Quiet Room achieves something rarer: it makes the reader feel the weight of isolation while remaining, somehow, a pleasure to read.

The novel follows three residents of a single apartment block in a large city over the course of one winter: a recently widowed retired teacher, a young delivery driver who has moved from abroad and a successful lawyer who works from home and speaks to almost no one. Their lives barely intersect, yet each, in a different way, is profoundly alone.

What makes the book so effective is its restraint. Mendes never lectures; instead, she accumulates small, precisely observed details, an unanswered phone, a meal cooked for one, a greeting in a lift that goes unreturned, until their cumulative effect is almost unbearable. The decision to give each character a distinct narrative voice is equally successful, revealing how loneliness can look entirely different from the outside while feeling much the same within. If the novel has a weakness, it is a final chapter that ties the three stories together a little too neatly, as though the author lost her nerve at the last moment.

Could it change readers’ views? I believe it could. Many of us think of loneliness as an affliction of the elderly or the socially awkward; this novel quietly dismantles that assumption. More importantly, it makes the reader notice the people who share their own building, their own street, their own office. I finished it and, for the first time in years, knocked on my neighbour’s door.

That, perhaps, is the highest praise a novel about a social issue can receive.

Why it works

  • The outline is brief and focused on the issue, without retelling the plot.
  • The evaluation is detailed and specific, and includes a reasoned criticism of the ending.
  • A wide range of evaluative language: restraint, cumulative effect, quietly dismantles, lost her nerve.
  • The personal anecdote at the end makes the answer to the third question convincing.

The Museum of the Sea: dusty treasures, brilliantly reborn

I confess I approached the newly reopened Museum of the Sea with low expectations. My memories of it, from a school trip many years ago, were of gloomy rooms, glass cases crammed with shells and labels written in a tiny font that no child could reach, let alone read. I expected a fresh coat of paint on the same tired collection.

I could not have been more wrong. The museum has been transformed by a simple but radical decision: to tell stories rather than display objects. The first gallery, for instance, recreates the deck of a nineteenth-century fishing boat, complete with the sound of creaking timber and the voices of fishermen reading from their own letters. Elsewhere, a single whale skeleton, suspended from the ceiling, replaces dozens of cluttered cases, and the effect is genuinely breathtaking. Interactive screens are used sparingly and well, offering depth to those who want it without distracting those who do not.

Not everything works. The final room, devoted to ocean pollution, abandons the museum’s subtle approach in favour of rather heavy-handed messaging, and the café, with its plastic cups, sits awkwardly with the warnings visitors have just been reading.

Who would benefit most from a visit? Families are the obvious winners: my nine-year-old nephew had to be dragged away from the virtual dive. However, the museum also rewards adults with an interest in history, since the personal testimonies bring a vanished way of life vividly back to life. Only those hoping for a traditional, comprehensive collection of specimens might leave a little disappointed.

All in all, this is a superb example of how an old institution can reinvent itself, and well worth a morning of anyone’s time.
