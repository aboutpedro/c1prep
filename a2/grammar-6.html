Grammar

Modal verbs

Each set has 10 sentences. Follow the instructions above the sentences, then press Check answers.

Modal verbs like can, must and should add meaning to another verb: ability, permission, obligation or advice. They’re easy to use because they never change: no -s, no to after them, and no do in questions.

The rules for every modal

  • She can swim. Not: She cans swim / She can to swim.
  • Should I call her? Not: Do I should call her?
  • You mustn’t touch that.

Careful: have to is different: it works like a normal verb. She has to work. Do you have to go? I didn’t have to pay.

can and could

Use can for ability now and could for ability in the past. Both are also used to ask for permission or for help. Could sounds more polite.

The basics

  • I can speak three languages, but I can’t drive.
  • When I was five, I could read. I couldn’t swim.
  • Can I open the window?
  • Could you help me, please?

You might also see

  • I can’t find my keys anywhere.
  • Could you tell me where the station is?
  • We could go to the cinema tonight.A suggestion.

Must and have to are very similar. Must is common on signs and for things the speaker feels are important; have to is common for rules and routines. For the past, use had to.

The basics

  • You must be quiet in the library.
  • She has to get up at five every day.
  • It’s Sunday, so I don’t have to work.

You might also see

  • The bus didn’t come, so I had to walk.
  • Do you have to work on Saturdays?
  • I must call my mum. It’s her birthday.

Careful: mustn’t and don’t have to are very different. You mustn’t swim here = it’s dangerous or not allowed. You don’t have to swim = it’s your choice.

should and shouldn’t

Use should to give advice or say what you think is a good idea.

  • You should see a doctor.
  • You shouldn’t eat so much sugar.
  • What should I wear to the party?
  • I think you should say sorry.
