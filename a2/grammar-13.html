Grammar

Adverbs and word order

Each set has 10 sentences. Follow the instructions above the sentences, then press Check answers.

Adjectives describe people and things: a careful driver. Adverbs describe how, how often, where or when something happens: She drives carefully. Most adverbs end in -ly, but a few important ones don’t.

Making adverbs

most words: + lyslow → slowly · quiet → quietly · careful → carefully
-y → ilyhappy → happily · easy → easily · heavy → heavily
-le → lyterrible → terribly · possible → possibly
no changefast → fast · hard → hard · late → late · early → early
irregulargood → well

The basics

  • She’s a careful driver. → She drives carefully.
  • He’s a good cook. → He cooks well.
  • They work very hard.

You might also see

  • This soup tastes good. You look tired.After look, feel, taste, smell, sound and seem, use an adjective.
  • I’ve been very busy lately.Lately = recently. Hardly = almost not. They aren’t the adverbs of late and hard.

How often?

These adverbs go before the main verb, but after be:

  • I always get up at seven.
  • She’s never late.
  • We don’t usually eat meat.after don’t / doesn’t
  • I can never remember his name. I’ve never been to Japan.after can, have, will…

You might also see

  • I go swimming three times a week.Longer phrases go at the end: once a month, twice a year, every day.
  • Sometimes I work from home.Sometimes and usually can also go at the start.

Word order

Don’t put an adverb between the verb and its object. The usual order at the end of a sentence is how → where → when.

  • She speaks English very well.Not: She speaks very well English.
  • I go to school by bus every day.
  • I met Ana in the park yesterday.

Careful: really, still, just and also work like always: I really like it. She’s still asleep.
