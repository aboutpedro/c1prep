The CEFR levels, A1 to C2

Every level on this site follows the CEFR, the Common European Framework of Reference for Languages. It describes what a learner can actually do with a language at six levels, from A1 (first words) to C2 (close to an educated native speaker).

The framework was published by the Council of Europe in 2001 and is now used for English all over the world: by schools, universities, employers and every Cambridge English exam. The six levels sit in three bands:

  • A1 and A2: basic user. You can deal with simple, everyday situations.
  • B1 and B2: independent user. You can manage on your own: travel, work, study and talk about most topics.
  • C1 and C2: proficient user. You use English easily and precisely, at work and in study, with very few limits.

A level is not only about grammar. It is about what you can understand, say and write. Below, each level has a short description, what a learner can do, and an example of the English a learner at that level produces.

Basic user

A1 BeginnerFirst steps in English

You know familiar words and very basic phrases about yourself and the world right around you. You can take part in a simple conversation if the other person speaks slowly, clearly and is ready to help.

  • Understand: names, numbers, prices, times and short signs, such as Open, No parking or Exit.
  • Speak: say who you are, where you live and what you like; ask and answer simple questions.
  • Write: fill in a form with your name, age and address; write a short postcard.
  • Grammar you meet: be, have got, the present simple, there is / there are, can, plurals, this / that.

Example: introducing yourself

Hello! My name is Ana. I am 14 years old. I am from Brazil. I live in Curitiba with my mother and my brother. I like music and pizza. I don’t like rain.

About 90–100 hours of study from zero. Practise A1

A2 ElementaryEveryday situations

You can handle simple, routine tasks that need a direct exchange of information: shopping, asking the way, talking about your family, your job or your weekend. You can describe your past and your plans in simple terms.

  • Understand: short, clear messages and announcements; simple everyday texts such as menus, timetables, adverts and short emails.
  • Speak: buy a ticket, order food, make an arrangement, talk about what you did last weekend.
  • Write: a short message or email to a friend: thanks, an invitation, a change of plan.
  • Grammar you meet: the past simple, the present continuous, going to, comparatives and superlatives, some / any, countable and uncountable nouns.

Example: a message to a friend

Hi Sam, thanks for your email. Last Saturday I went to the beach with my cousins. The weather was hot and we swam in the sea. Next weekend I’m going to visit my grandmother. Do you want to come to the cinema on Friday? See you!

About 180–200 hours from zero. Cambridge exam: A2 Key (KET). Practise A2

Independent user

B1 IntermediateGetting by on your own

You understand the main points of clear, standard English on familiar topics: work, school, free time. You can deal with most situations when travelling, and you can link sentences to tell a story, describe an experience, or give your opinion with simple reasons.

  • Understand: the main points of radio or TV on familiar subjects; everyday texts and personal letters about events, feelings and wishes.
  • Speak: join a conversation on a familiar topic without preparing; explain a problem; describe your hopes and plans.
  • Write: a simple, connected text: an email, a short story, an article for a school magazine.
  • Grammar you meet: the present perfect, the past continuous, first and second conditionals, the passive, modal verbs of obligation, relative clauses.

Example: giving an opinion

I think students should have less homework. When we have too much, we don’t have time for sport or for our families, and we feel tired at school the next day. However, some homework is useful because it helps us to remember what we learned in class.

About 350–400 hours from zero. Cambridge exam: B1 Preliminary (PET). Practise B1

B2 Upper intermediateComfortable and fluent

You understand the main ideas of complex texts, including technical discussion in your own field. You can talk with native speakers fluently enough that neither side has to make an effort, and you can explain a point of view on a current issue, with the advantages and disadvantages of different options.

  • Understand: long talks and arguments on familiar topics; most TV news and films in standard English; newspaper articles where the writer takes a position.
  • Speak: take an active part in a discussion, give reasons, react to other people’s ideas.
  • Write: clear, detailed essays, reviews, reports and formal emails that argue for or against something.
  • Grammar you meet: third and mixed conditionals, wish / if only, reported speech, modals of deduction (must have, can’t have), the passive in all tenses, linking words such as although and despite.

Example: part of an essay

Although working from home saves time and money, it is not the right choice for everyone. Many people find it harder to separate their job from their private life, and younger employees may miss the chance to learn from more experienced colleagues. If companies had considered these problems earlier, the move to remote work might have been smoother.

About 500–600 hours from zero. Cambridge exam: B2 First (FCE). Practise B2

Proficient user

C1 AdvancedFlexible and effective

You understand a wide range of long, demanding texts and notice meaning that is implied, not stated. You express yourself fluently and spontaneously, without obviously searching for words, and use English flexibly for social, academic and professional purposes.

  • Understand: long, complex texts, lectures and films without much effort, including irony, attitude and tone.
  • Speak: present complex ideas clearly, structure a long answer, and adapt your style to the situation.
  • Write: well-organised, detailed texts on complex subjects: essays, proposals, reports and formal letters, in the right register.
  • Grammar you meet: inversion (Not only…, Had I known…), cleft sentences, participle clauses, advanced passives (He is said to…), and a wide range of collocations and phrasal verbs.

Example: part of a proposal

Not only would a longer lunch break improve staff morale, but it would also, in all likelihood, reduce the number of sick days taken each year. What employees value most, judging by the survey, is a chance to step away from their screens. Having weighed up the costs, I would strongly recommend a three-month trial.

About 700–800 hours from zero. Cambridge exam: C1 Advanced (CAE). Practise C1

C2 ProficiencyNear-native mastery

You understand almost everything you hear or read with ease. You can summarise information from different sources into one clear account, and you express yourself spontaneously, very fluently and precisely, telling apart fine shades of meaning even in complex situations.

  • Understand: any kind of spoken English, even fast; abstract, complex or very idiomatic texts, such as literary writing and specialist articles.
  • Speak: take part in any conversation with ease, use idioms naturally, and rephrase smoothly when you hit a difficulty.
  • Write: complex, elegant texts in a style that fits the reader, including critical reviews and summaries of other texts.
  • Grammar and vocabulary you meet: the full range of structures, used for effect; idioms, fixed phrases, subtle differences between near-synonyms (reluctant, loath, averse) and register.

Example: part of a review

Rarely has a debut novel been so assured. The author resists the temptation to tie up every loose end, and the book is all the better for it: what lingers is not the plot, which is slight, but the quiet, unsparing portrait of a family that has long since run out of things to say to one another.

About 1,000–1,200 hours from zero. Cambridge exam: C2 Proficiency (CPE). Practise C2

Which level are you?

Most people are not at one level in everything: you may read at B2 but speak at B1. Take the level test (about 15 minutes) and it will suggest where to start.
